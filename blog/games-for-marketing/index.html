Build Them a Game: Why Playable Beats Scrollable

By Ray Wolters, KWB Media · November 10, 2026

Here's a confession from a guy who makes videos for a living: nobody has ever lingered on a website because of the About Us page. Not once. But put a tiny game on a page, and people who were about to leave suddenly need to beat their own high score.

For years that trick belonged to companies with a game studio and a budget to match. Not anymore. We build games now, with AI doing a lot of the heavy lifting, and I'd like to make the case that your business should consider one too. Or at least that you should stop scrolling past the idea like it's a pop-up ad.

Why a game keeps people on the page

A video asks someone to watch. A game asks them to do something, and humans have a weird, powerful need to finish what they've started. "Just one more try" is the most profitable sentence in the history of attention.

There's data behind the instinct, with a couple of caveats I'll get to. Mediafly's analysis, as reported by the Content Marketing Institute, found interactive content drew 52.6% more engagement than static content, and people spent about 13 minutes with it versus 8.5 minutes for static. Outgrow, a company that sells interactive tools (so, grain of salt), reports that interactive pages average roughly 3 times the time on page of a typical website visit.

Interactive content: 52.6% more engagement and 13 minutes vs. 8.5 of attention than static content, per Mediafly's analysis.

Now the caveats. Most of these numbers come from companies that sell interactive content, and "interactive" mostly means quizzes and calculators, not games. I'm not going to hand you a made-up number saying our games boosted anyone's sales by 400%. What I can tell you is the mechanism makes sense, and it's cheap enough now that you can test it on your own site and watch your own analytics instead of taking my word for it.

Memorable beats clever

Think about the last ad you actually remember. Now think about the last game you played. One of those has your muscle memory involved. A game turns your brand into an experience instead of a sentence, and experiences stick. Nobody has ever said "I loved your tagline" and then spent twenty minutes with it.

It also gives people something to share. "Look at this ridiculous game my plumber made" is a text message. "Check out my plumber's website" is not.

The one we made for a podcast

The best proof isn't one of mine. KWB Media built a game with AI for Oh F*ck, Now What?, the podcast hosted by Olympic gold medal-winning basketball star Kara Wolters. The game is based on Kara herself, and it lives on her page as a fun way to give people a reason to stick around and check out the show. It's called "Can you score on the Big Girl?" It's one button. Time your shot right, and don't let the Big Girl swat it. If she does, the game tells you that you need a new career, which is on brand for a show about career pivots, comebacks and reinventing yourself.

Look at what that does. A podcast page has a hard job: get a stranger to stay long enough to click play. A one-button game gives them something to do in the first five seconds, a score to beat, and a reason to send it to a friend. It also captures the host's personality in a single interaction, which is more than most "About the Show" pages can manage in four paragraphs.

"But I can't make a video game"

A few years ago, that was true. A game meant a developer, a designer, weeks of work and a budget that made your accountant lie down on the floor. Today AI can write the code, help draft the art and generate the music and sound effects, and a person with a clear idea can direct all of it. We made the music and sound for Brand Fighter that way too.

That doesn't mean it's effortless. You still need a good idea, someone with taste, and a lot of playtesting, because AI is happy to hand you a game where the boss can't be hit. (Ask me how I know.) But the barrier fell from "hire a studio" to "have a conversation," and that changes who gets to play this game.

How to make one that actually helps your marketing

  • Keep it simple. One mechanic you can learn in five seconds beats ten you can't. See: the Big Girl.
  • Make it sound like you. The game should be your brand's personality, not a generic arcade game with a logo slapped on.
  • Give people a reason to come back. High scores, new levels, daily challenges. Retention lives here.
  • Put a next step at the end. When someone wins (or loses spectacularly), point them to something: a contact page, a sign-up, an episode. Our Brand Fighter victory screen points to our social media services. Subtle as a brick, but it works.
  • Measure it. Compare time on page and return visits before and after. If the numbers don't move, you've lost a little time, not a fortune.

The bottom line

Video is still the best way to show who you are, and I'll defend that until my last render. But a game is how you get someone to stay and play with it for a while. If your site currently asks people to read, scroll and leave, giving them something to do is a very good idea.
