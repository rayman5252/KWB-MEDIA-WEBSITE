The Pilgrims Almost Starved to Death Before the First Thanksgiving. Here's the Business Lesson.

By Ray Wolters, KWB Media · November 16, 2026

Every kid learns the same version of the first Thanksgiving: Pilgrims show up, have a rough winter, nice neighbors help out, everybody eats turkey and corn and calls it even. Sweet story. Also missing the part where the Pilgrims nearly starved themselves to death for three straight years, and it wasn't bad luck. It was the business plan.

I think about this every November, because it's one of the best, truest things I know about running a business, and it's sitting right there in American history class, buried under the construction-paper hats.

The plan that was supposed to work

When the Plymouth colonists set up shop, they didn't divide up land and tell every family to fend for themselves. Under the deal they'd struck with the investors back in London who financed the whole trip, everybody worked the land together, and whatever came off it went into one common store, split up equally among the colony. Didn't matter if you worked yourself ragged from sunup to sundown or showed up late and left early. Same cut for everyone.

On paper, that sounds fair. In practice, it nearly killed them. Three years running, 1620 through 1622, the harvests were thin and people went hungry. Governor William Bradford, who ran the colony and wrote down most of what we know about it, eventually admitted the quiet part out loud: the strongest, most able workers resented busting their backs so their output could be split evenly with people who weren't pulling the same weight. So, being human, plenty of people quietly stopped pulling much weight at all. You don't need a degree in economics to see where that goes. Everybody's problem becomes nobody's problem.

The fix nobody saw coming (except Bradford)

In 1623, after three hungry years, Bradford scrapped the shared-labor system and assigned every family its own parcel of land to farm for themselves. That single change turned the colony's worst years into its best harvest yet, and Plymouth didn't face famine again for the rest of his 30-plus years as governor.

Give people their own plot, let them keep what they grow, and make them personally responsible for feeding their own kids, and suddenly everyone's out there before dawn. Bradford himself said the new setup made all hands “very industrious,” so much more corn got planted than anyone expected. Funny how that works. (To be fair to the history nerds out there: the land technically still belonged to the London investors for a few more years. Nobody owned a deed. But the responsibility for the harvest moved from “everyone’s” to “yours,” and that's the part that mattered.)

I've run a business for a long time now, and I've watched this exact dynamic play out in miniature more times than I can count. The project that's “the whole team’s responsibility” is the project that nothing happens on. The task that's clearly, specifically yours is the one that gets done.

But don't skip the part about the help

Here's where people oversimplify this story into a “pull yourself up by your bootstraps, nobody helped us” speech, and that's not right either. Plymouth doesn't make it to 1623, or to any Thanksgiving at all, without outside help. The Wampanoag leader Massasoit struck a peace agreement with the colonists in the spring of 1621, and a Wampanoag man named Tisquantum, better known as Squanto, taught them how to actually grow food in that soil, including the trick of planting corn with fish as fertilizer. Without that alliance and that knowledge, there's no colony left standing to reform three years later. There's just no colony.

So the real lesson has two halves, and you need both of them. Get the help. Build the alliances. Learn from the people who already know how to do the thing you're trying to do, whether that's a mentor, a partner, a vendor, or somebody you hire because they're better at it than you are. That's not weakness, that's how every single person who's ever survived year one did it.

But the alliance gets you to the field. It doesn't plant your corn for you forever. At some point, the harvest has your name on it, and no amount of goodwill from your neighbors changes who's accountable for whether it comes in.

What this means on a random Tuesday at your business

If you're running a small or mid-sized business, you're probably living both halves of this story right now without the hats and buckles. You've got vendors, contractors, maybe a few employees, a banker, an accountant, people whose help you genuinely need and should keep leaning on. Good. Keep doing that. Nobody builds anything alone, and the people who insist they did are usually lying or exhausted.

But the marketing plan that's “something we’ll get to as a team” never gets gotten to. The social media page that “everybody” is supposed to be posting on sits dead for months, because everybody's responsibility is nobody's job. The business that waits for things to just work out, the way the colonists waited around hoping the common store would even itself out, ends up having the same three bad winters. Somebody has to own the parcel. Usually, that somebody is you.
