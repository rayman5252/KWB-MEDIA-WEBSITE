AI Video Got Shockingly Good. It Still Loses to a Guy With a Camera.

By Ray Wolters, KWB Media · November 16, 2026

Every few weeks somebody forwards me a video of a dog surfing a wave made of root beer, or a guy riding a dragon down Main Street, and asks the same question: "Ray, is this thing about to put you out of business?"

Fair question. I've spent real hours playing with these AI video generators, the ones that turn a sentence into eight seconds of footage that never happened. They're genuinely impressive. They're also not what the hype videos want you to think they are. Let's talk about both halves honestly, because that's the only way I know how to talk about anything.

The numbers don't lie: this stuff exploded

I'm not going to pretend AI video is a fad. It isn't. Video marketers using AI tools to create or edit their video jumped from 51% to 63% in a single year, according to Wyzowl's latest survey. That's not a niche experiment anymore, that's most of the industry.

The global market for AI video generation tools is already worth roughly $946 million in 2026 and growing over 20% a year, according to Grand View Research. This isn't a side project for these companies. It's a land grab.

So yes, the tools are real, they're everywhere, and ignoring them completely would be like ignoring email in 1998. But "everyone's using it" and "it works the way you think it works" are two very different sentences.

Here's the part nobody puts in the demo reel

This is the stat that made me sit up. Researchers from Syracuse University and Ipsos ran a controlled study: 20 real ad campaigns, each one made twice, once by actual humans and once by AI, from the same creative brief. They showed them to 3,000 people.

Here's the twist, and it's a good one: viewers mostly couldn't tell which was which. Only 13% felt confident an AI ad was AI, and the exact same 13% wrongly guessed a human-made ad was AI too. People are basically guessing blind.

And yet the AI-made ads still underperformed, by about 14% on short-term sales impact and 17% on long-term brand trust, according to the Harvard Business Review writeup of the study. Nobody could consciously spot the fake, but something in the back of their brain still didn't buy it. That's not a skill issue on the viewer's part. That's a "something is subtly off and I can't tell you what" issue, and it's exactly the kind of thing that tanks a campaign without anyone filing a complaint.

On top of that, close to two-thirds of U.S. consumers (63%, per Cint's research) think brands have a moral obligation to disclose when they've used AI in their marketing. People don't just have a gut reaction to AI content. Plenty of them actively want to know when they're looking at it.

It can also just... disappear

Here's a fun one for anybody thinking about building a whole content strategy around a single AI video app: OpenAI launched its standalone Sora video app with huge fanfare, and shut the whole thing down about six months later, citing the cost of running it. One day it's the biggest thing in tech, the next day the app's gone and the API's on a countdown clock.

I've been doing this long enough to remember a dozen “can’t-miss” platforms that missed. The tools in this space are changing monthly, pricing is changing monthly, and the company behind your favorite one might decide tomorrow that it's not worth the electric bill. Build your actual business on your own footage, your own brand and your own relationships. Treat the AI tool of the month like a rental car, not a foundation.

And the robots still can't do hands

This isn't just my grumpy opinion. A 2026 research benchmark called Physion-Eval had experts comb through AI-generated video clips looking for physics that don't add up, stuff like objects colliding wrong, timing that's slightly off, or things moving in ways nothing in the real world moves. They found a human-noticeable physics glitch in 83% of third-person clips and a staggering 93.5% of first-person clips. The AI video-critiquing software they tested against those same clips mostly missed what a human caught instantly.

Hands are still the classic tell, though the newer tools have gotten better. On-screen text inside a generated clip is still mostly a mess. And if you need the same character, product or storefront to look consistent across more than one shot, you're fighting the tool the whole way. None of that is a knock on the technology moving fast. It's just where it actually is right now, not where the sizzle reel says it is.

And here's the one nobody can argue around: no AI tool can film your actual storefront, your actual staff, or an actual happy customer. If your business lives anywhere other than inside a computer, at some point you need a camera that was physically present. AI can't manufacture proof that you exist.

So what's it actually good for?

Plenty, honestly. I use it, and I'd be lying if I said otherwise. Where it earns its keep:

  • Storyboards and concepting: showing a client roughly what a shot or an animated sequence will look like before anyone spends a dime on production.
  • Stylized and animated content: cartoon mascots, explainer animation, anything that was never trying to look like real footage in the first place.
  • Fast social cutdowns: text, captions, quick stylistic edits for the stuff that lives and dies in a six-second scroll.
  • B-roll that doesn't need to exist in the real world: abstract backgrounds, fantasy sequences, the kind of shot you'd never schedule a real crew for anyway.

Where it doesn't replace anything: your actual product, your actual face, your actual location, or the kind of testimonial that only works because the person saying it is a real human who really means it.
