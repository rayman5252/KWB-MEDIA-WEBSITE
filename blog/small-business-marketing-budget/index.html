How Much Should a Small Business Spend on Marketing in 2026?

By Ray Wolters, KWB Media · September 25, 2026

Every so often a business owner asks me, "Ray, how much should I actually be spending on marketing?" And every time, I watch them brace for a number that's going to ruin their week.

Here's the good news and the bad news. The good news: it's a smaller number than you think. The bad news: it's a bigger number than you're currently spending, because if you're anything like most small businesses, you're currently spending somewhere between "not much" and "what's marketing."

Let's fix that with some actual math instead of vibes.

What the big guys spend

Marketing budgets at large companies averaged 7.8% of company revenue in 2026, according to Gartner's annual CMO Spend Survey. The broader CMO Survey out of Duke's Fuqua School of Business, which polls a wider mix of company sizes, puts the average even higher at 9.0%.

So somewhere between 7.8% and 9%, most of the marketing leaders in America agree that's roughly the right slice of revenue to hand over to marketing. And here's the part that should make you feel better about writing that check: even at those levels, 56% of CMOs say they still don't have enough budget to do everything they're supposed to do. Nobody feels flush. Not even the companies with actual marketing departments.

What small businesses actually spend

Now here's the gap. A survey of small businesses by UENI found the single most common marketing budget was $1 to $50 a month, picked by nearly 40% of respondents. Another 26% planned to spend nothing. Zero dollars. Not a typo.

Think about that for a second. The companies with the resources to measure everything down to the decimal point are putting 8 or 9 cents of every revenue dollar into marketing. And a quarter of small businesses are putting in a dollar amount best described as "whatever's in the couch cushions." That's not a strategy, that's hoping.

I get why it happens. When cash is tight, marketing feels optional in a way that payroll and rent don't. Nobody shows up at your door demanding the marketing invoice. But that's exactly the trap: the businesses that skip marketing when things are slow are the ones that stay slow.

So what's the real number for a small business?

You don't need to hit 9% overnight, and you probably shouldn't try. But there's a longstanding small business rule of thumb, echoed by the Small Business Administration, that lands right around 7% to 8% of gross revenue for an established small business with healthy margins. If your margins are thinner, or you're a newer business still trying to get your name out there, bump that up toward 10% to 12%. You spend more to get known, then ease off once people already know who you are.

Put in real numbers: a business doing $500,000 a year should be looking at roughly $35,000 to $40,000 annually on marketing. Spread across twelve months, that's not some untouchable enterprise budget. That's a video project, some social media management, a bit of paid advertising and maybe a trade show video, all in the same year.

Still sounds like a lot? Compare it to what you already spend keeping the lights on, insuring the building or replacing equipment that breaks. Marketing is the thing that makes sure people actually walk through the door to see all that stuff you're paying to maintain.

Where to actually put the money

I'm not going to tell you to blow your whole budget on video, even though that's what we do here. A real marketing budget is a mix. Here's roughly how I'd think about it if I were starting from a low or zero number and building up:

  1. Fix the foundation first. A website that loads fast, looks legit and actually explains what you do. This is the thing every other dollar you spend eventually points back to.
  2. Get one solid piece of video. A 60 to 90 second brand or explainer video does more heavy lifting than almost anything else on this list, and it works for you around the clock without asking for a raise.
  3. Cut that video into content. One shoot day can become a month of short clips for social media. This is the cheapest way to stretch a budget, because you're not paying for a new shoot every time you need a new post.
  4. Put a little behind ads. Even a modest amount pointed at the right audience beats posting into the void and hoping the algorithm notices you.
  5. Leave room to test. Don't spend every dollar on one channel. Try something small, see what actually brings in customers, then put more money where it's working.

The businesses that skip marketing entirely

I've seen this pattern more times than I can count: a business does great work, has loyal customers and grows for years on word of mouth alone. Then the market shifts, a competitor with a real marketing plan moves in, and suddenly word of mouth isn't cutting it. By the time they call, they're not asking for a marketing budget anymore. They're asking for a miracle. Miracles cost more than 8% of revenue, and I can't guarantee delivery.

The businesses that treat marketing like a real line item, even a modest one, don't have that problem. They're not scrambling. They're just steadily staying in front of the people who might buy from them, which is, in the end, the entire point.

The bottom line

You don't need a Fortune 500 budget. You need a number you actually commit to, spent on things that compound: a good website, real video, and the discipline to keep showing up in front of customers instead of disappearing for months at a time. Start smaller than 8% if you have to. Just don't start at zero.
